# Cooperative Asymmetric Board Game Concept

## Working Concept

A cooperative, app-assisted board game in which every player participates in the same shared world but interacts with that world through a fundamentally different gameplay system.

Rather than every character using the same underlying mechanics with different abilities, each player effectively plays a different style of game.

One player may manage dice.

Another may manage a deck of cards.

Another may use a bag-building and push-your-luck system.

Another may operate through action placement and systems management.

Despite those differences, all players interact with the same map, missions, enemies, shared resources, technology/capability progression, and objectives.

The intent is to create the feeling that several different board games are operating together as one cooperative experience.

A key principle is:

> **Rounds belong to the team. Actions belong to players.**

The game should emphasize simultaneous thinking, information sharing, coordinated actions, personal sacrifice, and interaction between the different player engines rather than traditional isolated player turns.

---

# 1. Core Design Philosophy

The central idea is **mechanical asymmetry rather than simply character asymmetry**.

Many games provide different characters with different abilities while keeping the underlying gameplay essentially identical.

This game takes asymmetry further.

Different players should have genuinely different relationships with:

- risk
- probability
- planning
- resources
- timing
- information
- optimization
- uncertainty

For example:

- The dice player reacts to uncertain results.
- The card player plans combinations and manages future options.
- The bag player manages escalating risk.
- The systems player optimizes a largely deterministic machine.

These players are solving the same problems, but they think about those problems differently.

The game therefore becomes partly about understanding how your teammates operate.

---

# 2. Prototype Theme: Palladium Rifts

For initial design and prototyping, the game uses the **Palladium Rifts** universe as its thematic reference.

Rifts is particularly suited to this concept because its world naturally combines radically different forms of power and character identity:

- advanced technology
- magic
- psionics
- cybernetics
- powered armour
- supernatural creatures
- dimensional travelers
- scholars
- soldiers
- mechanics
- explorers
- political factions

This makes radically asymmetric gameplay feel natural rather than arbitrary.

A Ley Line Walker, Operator, Juicer, Psychic, Scout, Cyborg, Techno-Wizard, and Robot Pilot should reasonably feel like they operate according to completely different rules.

### Important IP Note

Rifts is an existing Palladium Books intellectual property.

It works extremely well as a prototype setting and design framework.

A commercial release would eventually require either:

1. appropriate licensing/permission from Palladium Books, or
2. conversion to an original setting built around similar broad concepts such as dimensional disruption, post-apocalyptic civilization, magic, advanced technology, and supernatural forces.

The underlying game architecture should therefore remain setting-independent.

---

# 3. Cooperative Structure

The game is fundamentally cooperative.

Players win or lose as a group.

However, cooperation should **not** mean that every player's optimal decision is automatically whatever gives the party the highest mathematical benefit.

Instead, the game deliberately creates tension between:

- personal advancement
- personal objectives
- immediate survival
- helping another player
- advancing shared capabilities
- long-term team strategy

A player should frequently encounter situations where:

> “I know what would be best for the group, but doing it costs me something I genuinely want.”

That tension is one of the central emotional experiences of the game.

---

# 4. Personal Versus Team Progression

Characters have their own progression systems.

The team also has shared progression.

These systems intentionally compete for some of the same resources and opportunities.

For example, the group discovers an experimental Pre-Rifts power core.

A player might be able to:

### Keep It

Use it to unlock a powerful personal upgrade.

### Salvage It

Convert it into shared **Materiel**.

### Integrate It

Use it as **Power** toward a shared project.

### Study It

Convert it into **Knowledge**.

### Trade It

Convert it into **Influence**.

There should rarely be an obviously correct answer.

Keeping something personally should be genuinely attractive.

Giving it to the group should often be genuinely valuable.

---

# 5. Personal Objectives

Characters may also receive private or semi-private objectives.

These are similar in spirit to personal mission or battle-goal systems in games such as Gloomhaven, but they should primarily create temptation rather than outright betrayal.

Examples might include:

- personally discover three locations
- retain a rare artifact until mission end
- defeat a powerful enemy
- complete a mission without taking significant damage
- complete a particular type of action several times
- personally complete a research milestone
- protect a particular NPC
- avoid consuming a particular resource
- end the mission with a valuable item
- complete an objective without direct assistance

These objectives could provide:

- experience
- personal upgrades
- new character options
- reputation
- campaign progression
- narrative outcomes

The game should remain cooperative.

Personal objectives create hesitation and sacrifice rather than encouraging players to deliberately destroy the team's chances of success.

A core design principle is:

> **The cooperative move should often be obvious. The interesting question is what it costs you personally.**

# 6. Gameplay Engine Families

For the initial prototype, four gameplay-engine families are proposed.

These were chosen because they create fundamentally different decision-making experiences.

## Engine 1: Dice Allocation

### Player Experience

> “Give me uncertainty and let me improvise.”

The player rolls a pool of dice and then determines how to allocate those results.

The important decision is not simply whether a die roll succeeds.

Instead:

> “Given what I rolled, what is the best turn I can construct?”

Example roll:

- 6
- 5
- 4
- 2
- 1

Possible action slots might include:

- Attack — requires 4+
- Move — any value
- Defend — requires matching dice
- Assist — requires 3+
- Special Ability — requires a 6
- Team Progress — commit appropriate dice

### Possible Character Fits

- Juicer
- Combat Cyborg
- Mercenary
- Soldier
- Hunter
- Powered-armour combat specialist

### Personal Upgrades

Upgrades could:

- add dice
- improve dice
- provide rerolls
- manipulate die values
- introduce specialized dice
- create new allocation slots
- allow dice to be held between opportunities

### Cooperative Tension

A high-value die could be used for:

- a powerful personal attack

or

- contribution toward a critical team objective.

That makes cooperation immediately visible as a sacrifice.

---

# 7. Engine 2: Deck / Hand Management

### Player Experience

> “Let me construct combinations and plan ahead.”

The player operates through a relatively small personal deck.

Cards could represent:

- spells
- tactical actions
- research
- equipment
- psychic abilities
- preparation
- support
- environmental manipulation

The gameplay loop could involve:

- draw
- evaluate hand
- construct combinations
- play cards
- discard
- recycle or recover

Example:

`Ley Line + Channel + Lightning`

could generate a powerful offensive effect.

Alternatively:

`Research + Channel`

might generate Knowledge toward a team objective.

### Possible Character Fits

- Ley Line Walker
- Mystic
- Rogue Scientist
- psychic characters
- Coalition intelligence operative
- strategist

### Personal Progression

Players might:

- add new cards
- remove weaker cards
- improve cards
- modify draw size
- manipulate discard/recovery
- unlock new combinations

This player naturally thinks several actions or rounds ahead.

---

# 8. Engine 3: Bag Building / Push Your Luck

### Player Experience

> “Let me manage risk.”

The player has a personal bag containing tokens representing outcomes.

Possible token concepts include:

- Find
- Cache
- Signal
- Opportunity
- Hazard
- Complication
- Jackpot

These are **local gameplay symbols**, not universal shared resources.

The bag player draws tokens and decides how aggressively to continue pushing.

### Possible Character Fits

- Wilderness Scout
- Rift Explorer
- dimensional traveler
- Crazy
- scavenger
- prospector
- smuggler

### Example Conversion

The player might draw:

`Find + Cache`

which can resolve as:

- +1 Knowledge
- +1 Materiel

Or:

`Find + Find`

could produce:

- +2 Knowledge

`Cache + Cache`

might produce:

- +2 Materiel

`Signal + Find`

could:

- reveal additional information
- reduce fog
- generate Knowledge

`Signal + Signal`

could potentially generate Influence.

Two Hazards might create a negative consequence.

### Push-Your-Luck Identity

The bag character may be capable of generating unusually strong outcomes.

The cost is uncertainty.

They may continue drawing hoping to improve a result, while risking:

- injury
- threat generation
- mission complications
- loss of accumulated rewards
- contamination of their bag
- temporary penalties

### Bag Building

Personal progression modifies the bag.

For example:

- remove Hazard tokens
- add superior Find tokens
- add specialized tokens
- introduce wildcard tokens
- alter combination recipes
- ignore the first Hazard
- change how tokens convert into universal resources

### Personal Versus Group Tension

A particularly valuable token such as **Jackpot** might have two uses:

**Personal**

Return it to the bag permanently as a powerful future token.

**Team**

Cash it in immediately for a major contribution.

---

# 9. Solving Bag-Player Downtime

Traditional push-your-luck mechanics create a potential problem.

If one player repeatedly does:

> Draw → think → draw → think → draw → think

everyone else may spend too much time waiting.

The game should avoid this.

One solution is the game's intermingled action structure.

A bag pull can itself represent one opportunity.

For example:

1. Scout pulls.
2. Information or resources are revealed.
3. Other players act.
4. Play eventually returns to the Scout.
5. The Scout decides whether to pull again.

Push-your-luck therefore occurs across the shared action phase rather than as a long uninterrupted solo sequence.

This creates a useful side effect.

The situation may change between pulls.

The Scout's decision about whether to continue becomes contextual rather than isolated.

---

# 10. Engine 4: Action Placement / Systems Management

### Player Experience

> “Give me a constrained system and let me optimize it.”

This player operates through a personal systems board.

Unlike traditional worker placement, the action spaces primarily exist on the player's own character/system.

For example, an Operator might have:

- Fabrication
- Repair
- Analysis
- Power
- Support
- Logistics

They may have four action markers but six possible systems.

Different combinations could create bonuses.

Example:

`Fabrication + Analysis`

could manufacture advanced equipment.

`Power + Repair`

could restore a damaged vehicle.

`Analysis + Support`

could generate Knowledge and Progress toward a shared objective.

### Possible Character Fits

- Operator
- Techno-Wizard
- Robot Pilot
- mechanic
- systems engineer

### Personal Progression

Upgrades could:

- provide additional action markers
- increase action-space output
- create combination bonuses
- unlock new systems
- allow markers to remain between rounds
- automate some systems

This engine should be relatively deterministic compared with the bag and dice engines.

---

# 11. Future Engine: Programming / Action Queue

A fifth gameplay engine could eventually be introduced.

### Player Experience

> “Let me predict.”

The player programs several actions in advance.

Example:

`Move → Scan → Attack → Defend`

Those actions then resolve sequentially.

The interesting problem is forecasting what the battlefield will look like when each programmed action executes.

Possible fits include:

- robot pilot
- tactical commander
- advanced powered-armour operator
- AI-enhanced character
- autonomous drone controller

This engine should probably come after the first four are proven because programming systems can introduce substantial complexity.

---

# 12. Engine Families Versus Classes

Gameplay engine and class should not be the same thing.

Players should effectively be able to choose:

> **How do I like to play?**

and then:

> **Which character using that gameplay style do I want?**

For example:

## Card Engine

Possible future characters:

- Ley Line Walker
- Rogue Scientist
- Psychic
- Coalition Intelligence Officer

All use the same broad gameplay family but have completely different:

- abilities
- themes
- card sets
- progression paths
- information advantages
- universal-resource efficiencies

Only one card-engine character would normally participate in a party.

This preserves mechanical diversity.

A future game might therefore have twenty characters built around five gameplay families.

---

# 13. Universal Shared Currencies

The game needs a stable abstraction layer so future classes can interact with all existing content.

Five concepts were selected.

Four are bankable shared resources.

The fifth represents current effort.

## Materiel

Represents physical assets and useful things.

Examples:

- salvage
- armour
- electronics
- medicine
- ammunition supplies
- rare materials
- vehicle components
- pre-Rifts technology
- manufactured parts

Different classes acquire Materiel differently.

An Operator might fabricate it.

A Scout might salvage it.

A Mercenary might earn it.

A Diplomat might trade for it.

The shared game only understands:

**Materiel**

---

# 14. Power

Represents usable energy.

Possible sources include:

- reactor output
- fuel
- batteries
- PPE
- ley-line energy
- psychic energy
- dimensional energy
- supernatural sources

Local resources remain local.

For example:

A Ley Line Walker might personally track PPE.

A Psychic might personally track ISP.

A technological character might track reactor capacity.

Those systems do **not** become shared currencies.

Instead they may convert into the universal concept:

**Power**

The shared technology system should never require:

> 8 PPE

It might require:

> 4 Power

How that Power was generated is irrelevant to the shared game.

---

# 15. Knowledge

Represents understanding.

Examples include:

- reconnaissance
- magical lore
- scientific research
- recovered databases
- enemy weaknesses
- dimensional information
- maps
- schematics
- creature biology
- decoded communications
- historical information

Knowledge is particularly important because it connects directly to the fog-of-war system.

Exploration does not simply reveal the board.

It can generate useful Knowledge that may then be spent on shared capabilities or personal development.

---

# 16. Influence

Represents the team's ability to get other people or organizations to help.

Examples:

- reputation
- political access
- faction standing
- contacts
- favors
- trade relationships
- local support
- permissions
- specialists
- manpower
- allies

This allows the shared capability system to include more than technology.

Example projects might include:

### Mercenary Network

`4 Influence + 2 Knowledge`

### Local Militia

`3 Influence + 3 Materiel`

### Research Alliance

`3 Influence + 3 Knowledge`

### Safe Passage Agreement

`6 Influence`

---

# 17. Progress

Progress is different from the four universal currencies.

It represents:

> **Someone actually spending time and effort doing the work.**

Progress should generally **not be bankable**.

Example:

## Dimensional Stabilizer

Requires:

- 4 Materiel
- 5 Knowledge
- 3 Power
- 6 Progress

The team may already possess the necessary resources.

Someone still needs to spend valuable actions actually building or activating the system.

This is critical because it strengthens the personal-versus-team tension.

A character might have everything they need for a personal upgrade but instead spend two valuable actions producing Progress toward the shared project.

---

# 18. Shared Resource Design Rule

A fundamental design rule is:

> **No universal currency belongs to a class, and no shared project may require the presence of a particular class or gameplay engine.**

An Operator may be extremely efficient at producing Materiel.

That does not mean an Operator is required.

A party without a magic user can still obtain Power.

A party without a Scout can still obtain Knowledge.

Party composition changes:

- efficiency
- risk
- available strategies
- information quality

It should not determine whether a major part of the game is available at all.

---

# 19. Shared Capability / Technology System

The team collectively develops a shared capability network.

Calling this simply a "technology tree" may be too restrictive for Rifts.

The tree could include:

## Technology

- advanced sensors
- power systems
- robotics
- automated defenses
- weapons
- communications

## Magic

- wards
- dimensional protection
- ley-line manipulation
- magical defenses

## Psionic Capability

- mental defense
- sensing
- communication
- prediction

## Infrastructure

- bases
- transportation
- medical facilities
- logistics
- workshops
- communications networks

## Social / Faction Capability

- allies
- safe passage
- military support
- trade networks
- research partnerships

The party decides collectively which capabilities to pursue.

---

# 20. Shared Projects Require Multiple Types of Contribution

Projects should not simply require generic science points.

They may require combinations of:

- Materiel
- Power
- Knowledge
- Influence
- Progress

For example:

## Long-Range Surveillance Network

Requires:

- 5 Materiel
- 3 Knowledge
- 2 Power
- 4 Progress

When completed, it might:

- reduce fog of war
- reveal enemy movements earlier
- expand recon range
- improve mission preparation

A different branch might improve defenses.

Another might improve travel.

Another might provide magical protection.

The team therefore makes strategic choices about its long-term identity.

---

# 21. Individual Engines Generate the Ingredients

A core architectural principle is:

> **Individual engines generate the ingredients. The team decides what to build with them.**

The shared world should not know that one player rolled dice and another drew cards.

It only receives standardized outcomes.

This makes the game highly extensible.

A future character might use:

- dominoes
- tarot-like cards
- auctions
- drafting
- puzzles
- programming
- spatial tiles
- rhythm
- hidden bidding

As long as the engine ultimately produces valid universal interactions, it can plug into the existing game.

---

# 22. World Interaction Contract

The world itself should understand a relatively small number of universal verbs.

A preliminary interaction set could be:

## Move

Change physical location.

## Engage

Interact directly with:

- enemies
- systems
- NPCs
- structures
- hazards

Engage could represent:

- combat
- hacking
- repair
- negotiation
- manipulation

## Investigate

Gather information.

This may:

- reduce fog
- identify threats
- discover resources
- reveal hidden properties
- generate Knowledge

## Assist

Improve another player's action.

## Contribute

Commit resources or Progress to a shared objective.

## Acquire

Obtain:

- loot
- salvage
- resources
- equipment
- information

## Recover

Restore part of a player's personal engine.

These should remain broad.

Character engines determine how those interactions are produced.

---

# 23. Interaction Contract as an API

Conceptually, character engines should behave like plug-ins.

A character engine never directly modifies arbitrary world state.

Instead it generates universal outcomes.

For example:

`ENGAGE → 3 Progress`

or:

`INVESTIGATE → 2 Knowledge + Reveal Threat`

or:

`ASSIST Player 2 → +1 Progress`

The world then processes the result.

This separation is extremely important for future expansion.

---

# 24. Interaction Surfaces

The game can be understood as having four major interaction surfaces.

## Surface 1: Shared World

This is the common reality everyone can see.

It may contain:

- map
- settlements
- objectives
- enemies
- environmental threats
- party location
- vehicles
- major structures
- visible mission state

This is the closest equivalent to the physical board.

It answers:

> **What is happening in the world?**

---

# 25. Surface 2: Personal Console

Each player has a private interface representing their unique character engine.

For a dice player:

- dice pool
- allocation spaces
- personal abilities

For a card player:

- hand
- deck
- discard pile
- card combinations

For the bag player:

- bag state
- pulled tokens
- risk level
- available conversions

For the systems player:

- systems board
- action markers
- efficiencies
- upgrades

The personal console also contains:

- health
- status
- local resources
- personal upgrades
- personal objectives
- private information
- class-specific perception

It answers:

> **What can I do, and what does my character know?**

---

# 26. Surface 3: Team Surface

The team surface contains shared information such as:

- Materiel
- Power
- Knowledge
- Influence
- active shared projects
- capability tree
- mission objectives
- shared threat/escalation
- faction relationships
- major team conditions

It answers:

> **How are we doing as a group?**

---

# 27. Surface 4: Player-to-Player Interaction

Cooperation should not be limited to donating generic resources.

Players should be able to interact directly with one another's gameplay engines.

For example:

The card player might use:

### Tactical Insight

Change one of the dice player's dice by ±1.

The Operator might:

### Calibrate Equipment

Add a temporary positive token to the Scout's bag.

The Scout might:

### Target Identified

Reveal a weakness that improves another character's attack.

The dice player might commit a strong die to another player's action.

This creates the feeling that the different mini-games are physically reaching into one another.

---

# 28. Request-Driven Assistance

A player performing a difficult action may request assistance.

Example:

## Hack Coalition Security Door

Difficulty:

**High**

The acting player selects:

**Request Assistance**

Other players are then offered assistance options appropriate to their own engines.

### Dice Player

Commit a die of 4+.

### Card Player

Play a Technical or Insight card.

### Systems Player

Commit a Support marker.

### Bag Player

Perform a Support Draw.

All of these contribute to the same world interaction through different personal mechanics.

---

# 29. Bag Character Assistance

Bag assistance should retain the risk identity of the character.

For example, another player requests help.

The bag player chooses to make a Support Draw.

Possible results:

### Find

+1 Progress.

### Signal

Reveal additional useful information or reduce difficulty.

### Cache

No immediate bonus, but the Scout may retain something useful.

### Hazard

The Scout absorbs a consequence or creates additional risk.

### Jackpot

Large assistance bonus.

Helping another player therefore means:

> **“I am willing to take some risk for you.”**

---

# 30. Fog of War as Fog of Understanding

Fog of war should go beyond simply hiding map tiles.

Different characters should perceive different aspects of the same reality.

Consider an abandoned military installation.

Everyone sees:

> **Abandoned military facility**

A Scout might see:

> Movement detected. Approximately 4–7 biological entities.

A technology-oriented character might see:

> Pre-Rifts power system detected. Reactor may still be functioning.

A magic-sensitive character might see:

> Significant dimensional energy below the structure.

A psychic might see:

> Multiple minds detected. One unusually powerful presence.

Players are looking at the same world.

They possess different pieces of the truth.

The cooperative experience comes partly from assembling those pieces.

---

# 31. Information Quality Can Also Differ

Information asymmetry should not always be binary.

Players might receive information of different precision.

One character sees:

> Something hostile is nearby.

Another sees:

> Approximately three enemies.

Another sees:

> Three enemies, one heavily armoured.

Another sees:

> Three enemies. The armoured unit intends to attack next action phase.

This creates different levels of understanding rather than simply visible/invisible information.

---

# 32. Information Sharing Happens Early

Because information affects everyone's decisions, information distribution should occur toward the beginning of the round.

A preliminary round structure is:

1. World changes
2. Information is distributed
3. Players assess and communicate
4. Action phase begins
5. Consequences/world response
6. Round transition

This avoids players committing actions only to discover that another character possessed information that would have completely changed their decision.

---

# 33. Information Gathering Costs Something

Characters can often improve what the party knows.

For example:

- Scout performs Recon
- Operator performs Scan
- magic user senses dimensional activity
- psychic investigates minds

However, acquiring information should consume part of their personal capability.

For example, the bag character may make a Recon Pull early in the round.

That may reveal critical information.

But the tokens used during Recon may no longer be available during later action opportunities.

This creates another personal/team choice:

> Do I spend my engine helping everyone make better decisions, or preserve it for my own actions?

---

# 34. Team Rounds Rather Than Player Turns

Traditional structure:

> Player 1 turn  
> Player 2 turn  
> Player 3 turn  
> Player 4 turn

is probably not appropriate.

It creates downtime and reduces cooperation.

Instead, the game uses **team rounds**.

Within each round is an intermingled action phase.

Players:

- act
- hold
- assist
- react
- coordinate
- change plans

within the same overall period of game time.

The core principle is:

> **Rounds belong to the team. Actions belong to players.**

---

# 35. Intermingled Action Phase

A possible sequence might look like:

Scout performs Recon.

The Scout discovers:

> Coalition patrol approaching from the west.

The Soldier acts next and repositions.

The Operator works on restoring a reactor.

The Mage alters their original plan and prepares a defensive ability.

The Scout gets another opportunity and pushes their exploration further.

The Operator says:

> “I need one additional Progress to finish this.”

The Mage sacrifices a card to assist.

The reactor activates.

The team continues acting until players have exhausted or deliberately preserved their available capability.

This should feel like a squad operating simultaneously rather than characters politely waiting for one another.

---

# 36. Opportunities Instead of Turns

The internal rules may still need ordering.

A useful concept is an **Opportunity**.

During the action phase:

1. a player receives an opportunity
2. they act, hold, assist, or pass
3. another player receives an opportunity

The order does not necessarily need to remain fixed.

For example:

`Scout → Soldier → Operator → Mage → Scout → Mage → Soldier`

The group can choose sequencing based on tactical needs.

A possible restriction is:

> A player may not take another primary action until every other player who wishes to act has received an opportunity.

This prevents one person from monopolizing the phase while preserving flexible sequencing.

---

# 37. Holding Actions

Players should be allowed to intentionally preserve capabilities.

For example:

The Soldier keeps a die available.

The Mage keeps a reaction card.

The Operator leaves an action marker uncommitted.

The Scout stops drawing.

These held capabilities can respond to later developments.

That creates tactical conversations such as:

> “Don't go yet. Let me scan first.”

or:

> “I'll hold. If something attacks you, I can respond.”

or:

> “Break the shield first, then I'll attack.”

---

# 38. Reactions

Reactions could become an important mechanism.

Example card:

## Exploit Opening

When an ally engages an enemy, play this card to add +2 Progress.

Another player's action creates a window for someone else to contribute.

This makes the different gameplay engines interact in time rather than simply operating next to one another.

---

# 39. Different Engines Can Have Different Action Economies

Players do not necessarily require identical numbers of actions.

A dice character might have five dice.

A systems character might have four markers.

A card player might have a hand capable of three to six meaningful plays.

The bag player may have a theoretically larger number of pulls but escalating risk.

Balance should focus on:

- expected contribution
- flexibility
- risk
- information
- support value
- opportunity cost

rather than:

> “Everyone gets exactly four actions.”

---

# 40. Shared Resources Should Never Be Character Resources

This distinction must remain strict.

For example:

### Local Resources

- PPE
- ISP
- ammunition
- dice
- action markers
- cards
- bag tokens
- reactor capacity

remain inside a character engine.

### Universal Resources

- Materiel
- Power
- Knowledge
- Influence

exist in the shared game.

Local resources may generate universal resources.

The shared game never directly requires a character's local resource.

---

# 41. No Mandatory Classes

No mission, technology, or major system should require a specific class.

Instead, classes affect **how efficiently or safely something can be accomplished**.

For example:

A magic-oriented character may identify a Rift immediately.

Without one, the party may:

- use equipment
- acquire NPC support
- spend Knowledge
- take additional time
- accept greater uncertainty

Likewise, an Operator may repair something efficiently.

A party without an Operator can still repair it.

It may simply cost more Materiel, Progress, or time.

This ensures future team combinations remain viable.

---

# 42. App-Assisted Design

The app should enhance the tabletop experience rather than replace it.

The app is particularly valuable for:

- hidden information
- asymmetric perception
- private objectives
- fog of war
- scenario state
- enemy logic
- bookkeeping
- conditional events
- dynamic maps
- campaign state
- individualized interfaces

The computer can track complexity that would be cumbersome with physical components.

---

# 43. What the App Should Not Do

The app should know everything.

Players should still make the meaningful decisions.

The app should not become:

> “Press button and wait for the computer to play the game.”

Instead:

### The Shared Board Answers

> Where are we?  
> What is happening?  
> What can we interact with?

### The Personal App Answers

> What does my character know?  
> What can my character do?

### The Players Answer

> What should we do?

---

# 44. Digital-First Development

The game should initially be developed as a digital game.

This offers several advantages:

- inexpensive asset iteration
- rapid rules changes
- automated bookkeeping
- easier experimentation with unusual mechanics
- AI-generated temporary artwork
- procedural content experimentation
- automated test agents
- easier balancing
- hidden information management
- multiplayer testing

However, the game should still be **designed like a board game**.

Actions should remain:

- discrete
- understandable
- inspectable
- deterministic where appropriate
- rule-driven

The software should not hide the game's logic behind conventional video-game simulation.

---

# 45. Physical Metaphors Should Remain Visible

Even digitally, personal systems should visually resemble their tabletop equivalents.

The dice player should manipulate visible dice.

The card player should see an actual hand.

The bag player should visibly draw from a bag.

The systems player should physically place markers into systems.

This maintains the game's tabletop identity.

It also leaves open the possibility of a future physical/app-assisted edition.

# 46. Potential Future Physical Configuration

# 51. What the Prototype Needs to Prove

The prototype succeeds if four players feel like they are playing four mechanically different games while clearly participating in one cooperative experience.

We should see moments such as:

> “I really need this component for my personal upgrade, but if I contribute it we can activate the sensor network.”

or:

> “Don't act yet. Let me scan first.”

or:

> “I can help your roll, but I'll have to give up the card I was saving for my own upgrade.”

or:

> “I'll risk another bag pull. If it works I can finish the project for us.”

Those interactions are more important than having large amounts of content.

# 52. Current Design Constitution

The following principles should guide future development.

1. **Every player uses a different gameplay-engine family.**

2. **Only one player of each gameplay-engine family participates in a party.**

3. **Multiple classes may eventually exist within each gameplay family.**

4. **Players choose both a character fantasy and a preferred style of gameplay.**

5. **Personal mechanics may be unique. Shared requirements may never reference a personal mechanic.**

6. **Shared systems use universal resources: Materiel, Power, Knowledge, and Influence.**

7. **Progress represents current effort and is generally not bankable.**

8. **No universal currency belongs to a specific class.**

9. **No shared project requires a specific class or engine.**

10. **Party composition changes efficiency and strategy, not basic access to the game.**

11. **Individual engines generate ingredients; the team decides what to build with them.**

12. **Personal advancement competes with team advancement.**

13. **Helping the team should frequently carry a meaningful personal opportunity cost.**

14. **Personal objectives should create tension without encouraging deliberate griefing.**

15. **Information is intentionally asymmetric.**

16. **Different characters may perceive different aspects or levels of detail about the same world.**

17. **Information should usually be distributed before major action commitment.**

18. **Gathering additional information costs character capability.**

19. **Players should spend most of their time thinking and acting simultaneously rather than waiting.**

20. **Rounds belong to the team. Actions belong to players.**

21. **Actions during the main phase are intermingled rather than isolated into traditional player turns.**

22. **Players may act, hold, assist, react, or pass.**

23. **Different player engines do not need identical action counts.**

24. **Players should be able to directly interact with one another's engines.**

25. **Assistance should feel different depending on the assisting player's gameplay system.**

26. **The shared world communicates through a small universal interaction contract.**

27. **Character engines never need direct knowledge of the internal implementation of shared systems.**

28. **The app manages hidden state and complexity but does not make meaningful player decisions.**

29. **The digital implementation should retain visible tabletop metaphors.**

30. **The architecture should allow entirely new gameplay engines and classes to be introduced without rewriting existing missions or shared progression systems.**

# 53. Core Emotional Experience

At its heart, the game should create four simultaneous experiences.

## Mechanical Identity

> “My character genuinely plays differently from yours.”

## Cooperative Dependency

> “I can do things you can't do efficiently, and you can do things I can't.”

## Incomplete Understanding

> “None of us sees the entire situation alone.”

## Meaningful Sacrifice

> “I could use this for myself, but the team really needs it.”

When those four things are happening simultaneously, the game is delivering on its central concept.

---

# 54. Concise Concept Statement

> A cooperative, app-assisted board game where every player operates a fundamentally different gameplay engine while interacting with the same shared world. Players possess asymmetric information, generate universal team resources through their unique mechanics, and make repeated decisions between personal progression and group success. Team rounds use an intermingled action system that encourages communication, assistance, reactions, and coordinated sequencing rather than traditional isolated player turns.

# 55. Current State of the Design

The concept is now developed enough to move out of broad ideation and into game architecture and prototyping.

The largest foundational decisions have been made:

- cooperative structure
- mechanically asymmetric player engines
- expandable engine/class architecture
- universal shared economy
- shared capability progression
- personal/team resource tension
- asymmetric information
- app-assisted hidden state
- direct cross-engine assistance
- intermingled team action phases
- digital-first but board-game-oriented implementation

The next stage should focus on turning these principles into a **v0.1 playable ruleset** rather than adding additional high-level concepts.

The most useful next design work is likely:

1. Define the exact round structure.
2. Define the first mission.
3. Define the first four prototype characters.
4. Define the interaction-resolution rules.
5. Define health, damage, danger, and consequences.
6. Define the initial shared capability tree.
7. Define the first threat/enemy system.
